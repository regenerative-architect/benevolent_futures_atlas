01 -- Executive summary

Why these hundred, and why now

The portfolio is built around a simple, well-evidenced finding: safer futures are more likely when societies invest simultaneously in prevention, resilience, trustworthy institutions, broadly distributed capability, ecological stewardship, and mechanisms for learning from error. The UN Pact for the Future and Declaration on Future Generations frame present-day governance as carrying obligations toward future people; the Sendai Framework places prevention and preparedness at the centre of disaster-risk reduction; WHO's pandemic, One Health and antimicrobial-resistance work treats health security as inherently cross-sectoral; and current international AI-safety frameworks increasingly emphasise evaluation, monitoring and risk management rather than capability alone. [S02] [S01] [S04] [S07] [S13]

The highest-ranked ideas skew toward interventions with large established benefit surfaces and limited downside: universal multi-hazard warning, clean air and toxic-exposure reduction, secure-by-design technology, climate adaptation, anticipatory humanitarian action, social protection, public-health preparedness and locally trusted warning networks. More uncertain but potentially very high-leverage domains -- advanced-AI governance, cross-risk catastrophic-risk institutions -- remain important portfolio hedges even where near-term feasibility scores are lower. The scores throughout this atlas are transparent prioritisation heuristics, not empirical causal-effect estimates.

Portfolio principle. Do not fund only the highest numerical scores. A robust benevolent-futures strategy resembles a diversified safety portfolio: combine mature, scalable prevention with exploratory work on emerging risks; combine central capacity with distributed community agency; and avoid common-mode dependence on a single technology, ideology, institution, or forecast. The Futures Cascade engine (section 06) and the constellation visualiser (section 05) are both designed to make that portfolio *feel* like a set of live trade-offs rather than a static ranked list.
Evidence, not prophecy -- every score below is a heuristic you can and should recompute locally.
02 -- Evidence & assumptions

Evidence base, validation criteria, and working assumptions

The direction of travel is grounded in primary and official frameworks covering disaster-risk reduction, early warning, health security, antimicrobial resistance, AI governance, cybersecurity, climate science, biodiversity, resource use, open science, education, social protection, deliberative democracy and digital public infrastructure. Feasibility20%Uses mature methods, realistic governance and plausible financing with manageable coordination burden.Political and local conditions can dominate technical feasibility.
Safety / risk profile20%Downside is limited, detectable and controllable; intervention is reversible with clear stop conditions.A high safety score does not imply zero risk.
Scalability15%Can be replicated, federated or expanded without rapidly degrading quality.Scaling can destroy trust if local adaptation is ignored.
Equity15%Benefits reach disproportionately exposed groups and affected people have genuine voice, access and recourse.Distribution must be measured, not assumed.

Combined score = impact×6 + feasibility×4 + safety×4 + scalability×3 + equity×3, maximum 100. Only the 20 projects in the Priority Portfolio (section 04) were scored this way in the source material -- the atlas does not invent scores for the other 80; you'll see them marked "unscored" rather than assigned a fabricated number.

Working assumptions

Budget, geography and timeline are deliberately open-ended. "Low", "medium" and "high" effort are relative to the lead actor. The portfolio assumes lawful, rights-respecting implementation; informed participation where people or communities are directly affected; accessibility; meaningful routes for appeal and correction; Indigenous rights and data sovereignty where relevant; independent review for high-consequence systems; and avoidance of unnecessary sensitive-data collection. Community-resilience literature generally finds positive associations between social capital/community resilience and wellbeing or emergency response, while also noting measurement inconsistency and the need for stronger evaluation. How the combinatorics are counted, and how to add a new Mode

Each Mode is a small object with template-string pools (intro/clause/transition/closing) and a variantCount(k) function that multiplies the actual pool lengths together for k ingredients -- the number shown above the Generate button is computed from those pool sizes every time, never hard-coded. Versioning, canonicalisation caveats, and offline-security minimums

Use semantic versions: a major bump means readers may not safely interpret the file (this page rejects any schema_version whose major number isn't 1); a minor bump adds backwards-compatible fields; a patch fixes documentation only. This page's canonicalisation is a hand-rolled "sort object keys recursively, keep array order" routine -- it approximates RFC 8785 for the plain JSON this app produces, but has not been tested against the full JCS conformance suite, so do not treat a signature made here as verifiable by an unrelated RFC-8785-strict tool without checking first. The SHA-256 hash and, where available, the ECDSA-P256 signature are genuine (computed with the browser's native Web Crypto SubtleCrypto API, no external library) and are enough to (a) detect accidental corruption and (b) confirm a re-imported file matches what a given device previously exported.

Minimums this build actually enforces on import: reject wrong document_type; reject unknown major schema version; reject files over 2MB; cap party/selection arrays; drop any project id not in this atlas; escape every string before it reaches the DOM; rate-limit imports to five per minute; and surface (not hide) a hash or signature mismatch as a warning rather than silently trusting it.

09 -- Conclusion & sources

A starting sequence, and where the numbers came from

A safer future is unlikely to be summoned by a single breakthrough. The more defensible strategy is a mesh of benevolent capabilities: institutions that think beyond short budget cycles; governments that prevent predictable harm; schools that teach uncertainty and verification; businesses that internalise safety; communities with real mutual-aid capacity; individuals with durable civic habits; and consortia that make evidence and safeguards interoperable.

The practical starting sequence: choose a balanced portfolio of three to seven projects; establish baselines and rights/safety constraints before selecting solutions; begin with reversible pilots; pre-register success, equity and stop conditions; involve affected people in governance; publish evidence and near-misses; and only scale after independent review. For a first diversified portfolio, G03 or N01 covers acute resilience, G10 or N10 covers preventive health, E01 builds long-term capability, PV02 or G07 strengthens digital infrastructure, C01 builds local social resilience, and G06/X01 can serve as a frontier-risk hedge.

Open questions and limitations

The ranking is intentionally global and cannot encode local political legitimacy, institutional capacity, hazard exposure, fiscal constraints, cultural context or opportunity cost. Several proposals -- especially those concerning advanced AI, new data institutions, long-horizon governance and cross-risk coordination -- have thinner implementation evidence than mature interventions in public health, cybersecurity or disaster preparedness. Recompute the score with local evidence and stakeholder weighting before committing resources. This atlas avoids operational biological, cyber-offensive, weapons or other dual-use instructions; projects in adjacent domains are framed around prevention, governance, monitoring and resilience.
